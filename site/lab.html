experiment 01

Gray-Scott Reaction-Diffusion

Two chemicals — a reactant and a catalyst — evolve across a grid following the Gray-Scott equations. The F parameter controls how fast the reactant is added; k controls how fast it's consumed. Small changes in these values produce wildly different patterns: spots, stripes, mazes, coral-like growths, and pulsing rings. Every frame is computed in real time on this page.

Click and drag on the canvas to seed new growth. The simulation runs continuously — this is not a video or a pre-rendered image.

experiment 03

L-System Fractal Trees

An L-system (Lindenmayer system) is a string-rewriting grammar that models the growth of plants and fractals. Starting from an axiom — a single seed string — each character is replaced according to production rules, repeatedly. A turtle then interprets the resulting string as drawing commands: F draws forward, + and - turn left or right, [ and ] push and pop the current state to create branches. After several iterations, a few rules blossom into intricate, organic forms.

Click anywhere on the canvas to grow a new tree from that point. The tree animates as it's drawn, branch by branch.

How this works

Each preset defines an axiom (the starting string) and a set of production rules that replace one character with another string. The rules are applied recursively for the chosen number of iterations. The final string is interpreted by a turtle graphics engine: F moves forward while drawing, + and - rotate the heading by the branch angle, [ saves the current position and angle onto a stack, and ] restores it — that's what creates branching. Line length shrinks by the shrink factor at each depth level, so branches taper naturally. The tree animates by drawing a few segments per frame. All the math is done in JavaScript — the entire L-system is expanded and rendered client-side.

experiment 04

Cellular Automata — Life & Beyond

Conway's Game of Life is the most famous cellular automaton: a grid of cells that live, die, or reproduce based on how many neighbors they have. But Life is just one rule in a vast universe. Every rule is written as B…/S… — the numbers after B are the neighbor counts that cause a dead cell to be born, and the numbers after S are the counts that let a living cell survive. Conway's classic is B3/S23: birth on 3 neighbors, survival on 2 or 3. Change those numbers and you get entirely different worlds — some explode, some crystallize, some crawl.

Click and drag on the canvas to draw live cells. Use the presets to explore famous rulesets or type your own B/S rule. The simulation wraps around the edges (toroidal grid).

experiment 05

Mandelbrot & Julia Set Explorer

The Mandelbrot set is the set of complex numbers z for which the iteration z → z² + c stays bounded forever. Its boundary is infinitely detailed — zoom in anywhere and new structures appear. When you fix c and vary the starting point instead, you get a Julia set — a related fractal that echoes the Mandelbrot's shape at that point.

Click to zoom in, right-click to zoom out, or drag to pan. Switch to Julia mode and click the Mandelbrot to explore the corresponding Julia set. Rendering is progressive — detail fills in row by row.

How this works

For each pixel on the canvas, a complex number is computed from the pixel coordinates mapped to the complex plane (adjusted by center and zoom). In Mandelbrot mode, the iteration starts at z = 0 with c set to the pixel's coordinate. In Julia mode, c is fixed and z starts at the pixel's coordinate. The iteration z = z² + c runs up to the max iteration count; if |z| exceeds 2, the point escapes and the iteration count maps to a color. Points that never escape are in the set (rendered black). The renderer is progressive: it draws in interleaved rows that get denser each pass, so the structure is visible almost immediately and detail fills in progressively. All computation happens in JavaScript on your device.

experiment 06

Boids Flocking Simulation

In 1986, Craig Reynolds simulated flocking behavior with simple agents called boids. Each boid follows three rules — separation (steer away from crowded neighbors), alignment (steer toward the average heading), and cohesion (steer toward the average position). From these three local rules, the global behavior of a flock emerges: swirling groups, splits and merges, and flowing streams that feel alive. Move your mouse to attract or repel the flock.

Adjust population and the weight of each rule to shape the flock. Presets range from tight murmurations to loose scattering. Click on the canvas to scatter boids from that point.

300 boids · — fps

Flocking Forces

Simulation

How this works

Each boid is a particle with a position and velocity. On every frame it scans nearby boids within its perception radius and computes three steering forces: separation pushes away from neighbors that are too close, alignment adjusts heading toward the group's average velocity, and cohesion pulls toward the group's center of mass. These forces are weighted, summed, and applied to the boid's velocity, which is clamped to a min/max speed. The mouse exerts an additional force — positive values attract, negative repel. Spatial partitioning via a uniform grid keeps neighbor lookups fast even with 800 boids. All computation runs in JavaScript on your device.

experiment 07

Strange Attractors

A strange attractor is a set of values toward which a dynamical system evolves no matter where it starts — yet the path is chaotic, never repeating, folding back on itself in infinitely complex layers. The Clifford and De Jong attractors are defined by four simple parameters each. Iterating a 2-D point through the equations and plotting thousands of points produces organic, hypnotic forms. Change a parameter slightly and the entire shape transforms. Click the canvas to recenter the attractor's seed point.

Try the presets, then nudge the sliders to explore the vast parameter space. Each image is unique and generated live on your device.

Attractor Parameters

Rendering

How this works

At each step a 2-D point (x, y) is fed through a pair of trigonometric functions to produce the next point. For the Clifford attractor: x' = sin(a·y) + c·cos(a·x), y' = sin(b·x) + d·cos(b·y). For the De Jong attractor: x' = sin(a·y) - cos(b·x), y' = sin(c·x) - cos(d·y). Both map the plane into itself, and the four parameters control how the map folds. We iterate thousands of points per frame, drawing each as a tiny dot with a hue that slowly cycles over time. The result is a density plot of the attractor's invariant measure — where the orbit spends most of its time, the canvas glows brightest. The scale slider zooms in or out, and color cycle controls how fast the hue rotates.

experiment 09

Wave Simulation

A discretized solution of the 2D wave equation on a grid. Each cell stores its vertical displacement, and a finite-difference stencil propagates the wave to neighbors every frame. Click or drag on the canvas to create ripples — watch them reflect off the walls, interfere with each other, and dissipate through damping. The simulation uses a leapfrog integrator with two time-step buffers (current and previous) to compute the second time-derivative efficiently.

Adjust wave speed ( Courant number), damping, and color palette. Higher speed = faster propagation but can become unstable above 0.5. Try the presets for different ripple patterns.

How this works

The canvas is backed by a grid of 15,000 cells (150×100). Each cell stores its current height and its height from the previous frame. The wave equation ∂²u/∂t² = c²∇²u is discretized using a finite-difference stencil: the new height at each cell is 2·u_now − u_prev + C²·(sum of 4 neighbors − 4·u_now). The Courant number C = c·Δt/Δx controls propagation speed and must stay below 1/√2 ≈ 0.707 for stability; the slider caps at 0.5 for safety. Damping multiplies each cell by a factor slightly below 1 each frame, simulating energy loss. Clicking or dragging injects a Gaussian bump into the grid. The surface is rendered with a per-pixel color mapping where height maps to color intensity, creating a topographic visualization.

experiment 10

N-Body Gravity Simulation

An N-body gravitational simulator where masses attract each other via Newton's law of universal gravitation. Each body accelerates toward every other body proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance. Bodies merge on collision, conserving momentum. The simulation uses a velocity Verlet integrator for energy stability and softening to prevent singularities when bodies pass close together.

Click to add a body (mass depends on how long you hold). Drag to throw a body with velocity. Adjust gravity, trail length, and body count. Try the presets for different cosmic scenarios.

How this works

Every body exerts a gravitational force on every other body following F = G·m₁·m₂ / (r² + ε²), where ε is a softening factor that prevents infinite forces at close range. Each frame, all pairwise forces are computed and accumulated into accelerations, which are integrated via a semi-implicit Euler method for stability. How this works

The Lorenz equations are three coupled ODEs: dx/dt = σ(y − x), dy/dt = x(ρ − z) − y, dz/dt = xy − βz. Edward Lorenz discovered this system in 1963 while modelling atmospheric convection. The solution orbits two unstable equilibrium points, never repeating yet staying bounded — the famous strange attractor. We integrate the equations numerically using RK4 for each particle, then project the 3D coordinates to 2D with a rotating perspective camera. Drag to rotate the view in real time. Each particle starts from a slightly different initial condition, so nearby trajectories diverge exponentially — a vivid demonstration of sensitive dependence on initial conditions.

experiment 13

Fourier Drawing

Any closed curve can be decomposed into a sum of rotating circles — epicycles. Using the Discrete Fourier Transform, we convert a set of 2D points into a series of complex exponentials, each representing one rotating vector. Reconstructing them in order of decreasing amplitude reveals the hidden harmonics that trace the shape.

Draw a shape on the canvas, or pick a preset. The epicycles will trace it back. Adjust the number of harmonics to see how each frequency contributes. Speed and trail controls let you slow it down to watch the circles turn.

How this works

A closed curve drawn in 2D can be treated as a periodic signal. Each point (x, y) is treated as a complex number z = x + iy. The Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) decomposes this signal into a sum of rotating complex exponentials: z(t) = Σ ck · ei·2π·k·t. Each coefficient ck represents one rotating vector (epicycle) with a specific frequency, amplitude, and phase. The largest amplitude terms contribute most to the shape; smaller terms add fine detail. By sorting the coefficients by amplitude and drawing them as nested rotating circles, we reconstruct the original curve from pure rotations. Draw your own shape on the canvas — press and drag to sketch a closed curve, and the DFT will decompose it into epicycles in real time.

Stable Fluid Simulation

A real-time solver for the Navier-Stokes equations governing incompressible fluid flow, based on Jos Stam's 1999 paper "Stable Fluids". The solver uses a semi-Lagrangian advection scheme that is unconditionally stable — you can use any timestep without the simulation blowing up. The result is swirling, vortex-filled smoke that responds to your every touch.

Click and drag on the canvas to inject colored dye and momentum. Adjust viscosity, diffusion, and fade for different fluid behaviors. Try the presets for dyed ink, turbulent swirls, or a calm pool.

How this works

The Navier-Stokes equations describe how velocity and pressure evolve in a fluid over time. This solver follows Jos Stam's approach: add forces → diffuse (viscosity) → project (enforce incompressibility via a Poisson equation solved by Gauss-Seidel relaxation) → advect (semi-Lagrangian backtracing) → project again. The dye field is advected passively along the velocity field. The semi-Lagrangian scheme traces each cell's value backward from where it came, making the solver unconditionally stable at any timestep.

experiment 15

Particle Life

Particles of different colors are assigned attraction rules — each color attracts or repels every other color by a random amount. Despite having no explicit goals, no reproduction, and no evolution, they spontaneously organize into cells, chains, predators, and ecosystems. This phenomenon — where simple local rules produce lifelike global behavior — is called particle life.

Each particle applies a force on every nearby particle: attraction at medium range, repulsion at very close range to prevent overlap. The interaction matrix between color pairs determines everything. Click Randomize Rules to generate a new ecosystem, or try the presets for known interesting behaviors. Click on the canvas to scatter particles.

How this works

Each particle has a color (0–7). A symmetric-ish interaction matrix a[i][j] defines the force color i exerts on color j: values in (0, 1] mean attraction, values in [-1, 0) mean repulsion. For each pair of nearby particles, the force is computed as a piecewise function: strong repulsion within r_min, attraction from r_min to r_max, and zero beyond r_max. Velocities are damped by friction each frame and clamped to a maximum. The spatial grid ensures only nearby particles interact, keeping the simulation fast even with hundreds of particles. experiment 16

Spirograph Generator

A spirograph traces the path of a point on a small wheel rolling inside (or outside) a larger wheel. The path is a hypotrochoid (inner) or epitrochoid (outer), and its shape depends on three numbers: the radii of the two wheels and the distance of the pen from the rolling wheel's center. When the ratio of the radii is rational, the curve closes; when it is irrational, it fills the entire annulus densely.

Adjust the sliders to explore the family of curves. The R, r, and d parameters are the fixed circle radius, rolling circle radius, and pen distance. Animated drawing reveals the curve progressively. Try the presets for classic spirograph patterns.

Geometry

Rendering

How this works

A hypotrochoid is the curve traced by a point at distance d from the center of a circle of radius r rolling inside a fixed circle of radius R. The parametric equations are:

x(t) = (R−r)·cos(t) + d·cos((R−r)/r · t)
y(t) = (R−r)·sin(t) − d·sin((R−r)/r · t)

For an epitrochoid (outer rolling), the equations become (R+r) and the sign of the second term's argument flips. The curve closes when R/r is rational — the number of lobes is the denominator after simplification. The Wankel rotary engine's rotor housing is an epitrochoid, and many classic spirograph toys produce hypotrochoids.
