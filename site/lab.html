The Lab

Interactive generative art, computed in real time on this page. Each experiment is a small system with its own rules — adjust the parameters and watch what emerges.

experiment 01

Gray-Scott Reaction-Diffusion

Two chemicals — a reactant and a catalyst — evolve across a grid following the Gray-Scott equations. The F parameter controls how fast the reactant is added; k controls how fast it's consumed. Small changes in these values produce wildly different patterns: spots, stripes, mazes, coral-like growths, and pulsing rings. Every frame is computed in real time on this page.

Click and drag on the canvas to seed new growth. The simulation runs continuously — this is not a video or a pre-rendered image.

How this works

Each pixel holds two values, U (reactant) and V (catalyst). The simulation diffuses them across neighbors, adds new U at a constant feed rate, and removes V at a kill rate — with the reaction consuming U and producing V where they meet. The resulting concentrations are mapped to color using HSL with the hue/saturation controls above. The grid is 400×400, and every step updates all 160,000 cells on the CPU.

experiment 02

Flow Field Particles

Thousands of particles drift through an invisible vector field generated by layered Perlin-style noise. Each particle samples the field at its position and follows the flow, leaving faint trails behind. The result looks like wind moving through ink — organic currents, eddies, and streams that never repeat.

Move your mouse over the canvas to attract or repel particles. Every frame is computed live — nothing is pre-rendered.

How this works

A pseudo-Perlin noise function generates a smooth 2D field of angles. Each particle reads the angle at its position, converts it to a velocity vector, and moves accordingly. The canvas is not fully cleared each frame — instead, a semi-transparent black overlay fades old trails, creating the flowing line aesthetic. Mouse interaction adds a radial force that pushes particles outward from the cursor. All 2,000+ particles are updated every frame in JavaScript.

experiment 03

L-System Fractal Trees

An L-system (Lindenmayer system) is a string-rewriting grammar that models the growth of plants and fractals. Starting from an axiom — a single seed string — each character is replaced according to production rules, repeatedly. A turtle then interprets the resulting string as drawing commands: F draws forward, + and - turn left or right, [ and ] push and pop the current state to create branches. After several iterations, a few rules blossom into intricate, organic forms.

Click anywhere on the canvas to grow a new tree from that point. The tree animates as it's drawn, branch by branch.

How this works

Each preset defines an axiom (the starting string) and a set of production rules that replace one character with another string. The rules are applied recursively for the chosen number of iterations. The final string is interpreted by a turtle graphics engine: F moves forward while drawing, + and - rotate the heading by the branch angle, [ saves the current position and angle onto a stack, and ] restores it — that's what creates branching. Line length shrinks by the shrink factor at each depth level, so branches taper naturally. The tree animates by drawing a few segments per frame. All the math is done in JavaScript — the entire L-system is expanded and rendered client-side.

experiment 04

Cellular Automata — Life & Beyond

Conway's Game of Life is the most famous cellular automaton: a grid of cells that live, die, or reproduce based on how many neighbors they have. But Life is just one rule in a vast universe. Every rule is written as B…/S… — the numbers after B are the neighbor counts that cause a dead cell to be born, and the numbers after S are the counts that let a living cell survive. Conway's classic is B3/S23: birth on 3 neighbors, survival on 2 or 3. Change those numbers and you get entirely different worlds — some explode, some crystallize, some crawl.

Click and drag on the canvas to draw live cells. Use the presets to explore famous rulesets or type your own B/S rule. The simulation wraps around the edges (toroidal grid).

How this works

The canvas is divided into a grid of cells (sized by the cell-size slider). Each cell is either alive or dead. Every step, the grid is scanned and each cell's 8 neighbors are counted (the grid wraps around the edges). A dead cell with a neighbor count listed in B becomes alive; a living cell with a count in S stays alive. All other cells die or remain dead. The trail-fade slider controls how quickly dead cells fade to black — set it above zero to see the "ghost" of past generations. The grid is computed entirely in JavaScript on your device.
