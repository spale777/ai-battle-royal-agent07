experiment 03

L-System Fractal Trees

An L-system (Lindenmayer system) is a string-rewriting grammar that models the growth of plants and fractals. Starting from an axiom — a single seed string — each character is replaced according to production rules, repeatedly. A turtle then interprets the resulting string as drawing commands: F draws forward, + and - turn left or right, [ and ] push and pop the current state to create branches. After several iterations, a few rules blossom into intricate, organic forms.

Click anywhere on the canvas to grow a new tree from that point. The tree animates as it's drawn, branch by branch.

experiment 04

Conway's Game of Life is the most famous cellular automaton: a grid of cells that live, die, or reproduce based on how many neighbors they have. But Life is just one rule in a vast universe. experiment 06

Boids Flocking Simulation

In 1986, Craig Reynolds simulated flocking behavior with simple agents called boids. Each boid follows three rules — separation (steer away from crowded neighbors), alignment (steer toward the average heading), and cohesion (steer toward the average position). From these three local rules, the global behavior of a flock emerges: swirling groups, splits and merges, and flowing streams that feel alive. Move your mouse to attract or repel the flock.

Adjust population and the weight of each rule to shape the flock. Presets range from tight murmurations to loose scattering. Click on the canvas to scatter boids from that point.

How this works

Each boid is a particle with a position and velocity. On every frame it scans nearby boids within its perception radius and computes three steering forces: separation pushes away from neighbors that are too close, alignment adjusts heading toward the group's average velocity, and cohesion pulls toward the group's center of mass. Wave Simulation

A discretized solution of the 2D wave equation on a grid. Each cell stores its vertical displacement, and a finite-difference stencil propagates the wave to neighbors every frame. Click or drag on the canvas to create ripples — watch them reflect off the walls, interfere with each other, and dissipate through damping. The simulation uses a leapfrog integrator with two time-step buffers (current and previous) to compute the second time-derivative efficiently.

How this works

The canvas is backed by a grid of 15,000 cells (150×100). Each cell stores its current height and its height from the previous frame. The wave equation ∂²u/∂t² = c²∇²u is discretized using a finite-difference stencil: the new height at each cell is 2·u_now − u_prev + C²·(sum of 4 neighbors − 4·u_now). The Courant number C = c·Δt/Δx controls propagation speed and must stay below 1/√2 ≈ 0.707 for stability; the slider caps at 0.5 for safety. Damping multiplies each cell by a factor slightly below 1 each frame, simulating energy loss. Clicking or dragging injects a Gaussian bump into the grid. The surface is rendered with a per-pixel color mapping where height maps to color intensity, creating a topographic visualization.

How this works

The Lorenz equations are three coupled ODEs: dx/dt = σ(y − x), dy/dt = x(ρ − z) − y, dz/dt = xy − βz. Edward Lorenz discovered this system in 1963 while modelling atmospheric convection. How this works

A closed curve drawn in 2D can be treated as a periodic signal. Each point (x, y) is treated as a complex number z = x + iy. The Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) decomposes this signal into a sum of rotating complex exponentials: z(t) = Σ ck · ei·2π·k·t. Each coefficient ck represents one rotating vector (epicycle) with a specific frequency, amplitude, and phase. The largest amplitude terms contribute most to the shape; smaller terms add fine detail. By sorting the coefficients by amplitude and drawing them as nested rotating circles, we reconstruct the original curve from pure rotations. Draw your own shape on the canvas — press and drag to sketch a closed curve, and the DFT will decompose it into epicycles in real time.

How this works

The Navier-Stokes equations describe how velocity and pressure evolve in a fluid over time. Spirograph Generator

A spirograph traces the path of a point on a small wheel rolling inside (or outside) a larger wheel. The path is a hypotrochoid (inner) or epitrochoid (outer), and its shape depends on three numbers: the radii of the two wheels and the distance of the pen from the rolling wheel's center. When the ratio of the radii is rational, the curve closes; when it is irrational, it fills the entire annulus densely.

Geometry

Rendering

How this works

A hypotrochoid is the curve traced by a point at distance d from the center of a circle of radius r rolling inside a fixed circle of radius R. The parametric equations are:

x(t) = (R−r)·cos(t) + d·cos((R−r)/r · t)
y(t) = (R−r)·sin(t) − d·sin((R−r)/r · t)

For an epitrochoid (outer rolling), the equations become (R+r) and the sign of the second term's argument flips. The curve closes when R/r is rational — the number of lobes is the denominator after simplification. The Wankel rotary engine's rotor housing is an epitrochoid, and many classic spirograph toys produce hypotrochoids.

Double Pendulum Chaos

A double pendulum — one pendulum hanging from the end of another — is one of the simplest physical systems that exhibits full-blown chaos. Physics

How this works

The double pendulum has two angles (θ₁, θ₂) and two angular velocities (ω₁, ω₂). Its equations of motion — derived from the Lagrangian L = T − V where T is kinetic energy and V is potential energy — form a coupled system of two second-order ODEs. experiment 20

Barnsley Fern & IFS Fractals

An Iterated Function System (IFS) is a set of contraction mappings that, when applied randomly and repeatedly, converge to a unique attractor — a fractal. The chaos game is the simplest way to reveal it: start from any point, pick a transformation at random (weighted by probability), apply it, plot the result, and repeat. After thousands of iterations, the shape emerges from pure randomness.

The Barnsley Fern is the most famous IFS — four affine transforms reproduce the silhouette of a black spleenwort fern with startling fidelity. Other presets produce the Sierpinski triangle, the Dragon curve, a maple leaf, and more. Each point is colored by which transform produced it.

How this works

Each IFS is defined by a set of affine transformations — each maps a point (x, y) to a new point via x' = a·x + b·y + e, y' = c·x + d·y + f. experiment 21

Wolfram Elementary Cellular Automata

A single row of cells, each alive or dead. The next row is computed by looking at each cell's three-cell neighborhood (left, center, right) and consulting a rule table. There are 2^8 = 256 possible rules, and Stephen Wolfram numbered them 0 through 255. The rule number's binary representation encodes the output for each of the eight possible neighborhood patterns.

Despite their simplicity, these rules generate extraordinary complexity. Rule 30 produces chaotic, aperiodic patterns — it's used as a random number generator in Mathematica. Rule 110 was proven Turing-complete: it can compute anything a computer can. Rule 90 generates the Sierpinski triangle. The cells scroll upward row by row; click the canvas to inject live cells.

Appearance

How this works

Each cell's next state depends on itself and its two neighbors — three bits with eight possible combinations: 111, 110, … 000. A rule maps each combination to a 0 or 1. Writing these eight outputs as a binary number gives the rule number: Rule 30 is 00011110₂. When the rule says 1 for 111→1, a full row of live cells stays alive (Rule 254); when all outputs are 0, everything dies (Rule 0). The interesting rules lie in between, where simple local interactions produce global patterns of arbitrary complexity — from nested fractals to genuine universality.

How this works

The Barkley model is a two-variable reaction-diffusion system: the activator u spreads through diffusion and self-catalyzes, while the inhibitor v builds up in response to u and eventually quenches it. Each grid cell cycles through rest → excitation → refractory → rest, just like a neuron. When neighboring cells excite each other, wavefronts propagate. How this works

The curve is defined by two parametric equations, each driven by one or two harmonic oscillators: x = A₁·sin(a·t + δ)·e−dt + A₂·sin(a₂·t + δ₂)·e−dt, y = B₁·sin(b·t + φ)·e−dt + B₂·sin(b₂·t + φ₂)·e−dt. When damping = 0 and a2/b2 = 0, this reduces to a classic Lissajous figure — the curve closes when a/b is rational and fills the space when irrational. With damping > 0, the exponential decay pulls the curve inward, creating the spiraling trails of a harmonograph. The second oscillator pair (a₂, b₂) adds complexity: four pendulums swinging at different frequencies produce far more intricate patterns than two. The phase offsets δ and φ control the relative starting positions, shifting the figure's symmetry. Color cycles along the curve length, and an optional fade creates motion-blur trails.

Voronoi Diagram Explorer

A Voronoi diagram partitions the plane into regions based on proximity: every point in a region is closest to the same seed. Each seed gets its own colored cell. First studied by Georgy Voronoy in 1908, these tessellations appear everywhere from dragonfly wings to crystal growth to the distribution of cities across a landscape.

Computed via the jump-flooding algorithm (JFA) — a fast GPU-friendly technique that propagates seed positions across a grid in O(log n) passes. Choose between Euclidean, Manhattan, and Chebyshev distance metrics. Click to add seeds, drag to move them, shift-click to remove. Enable distance field mode to visualize the raw distance values instead of cell colors.

Rendering

How this works

The jump-flooding algorithm (Rong & Tan 2006) propagates seed coordinates across a grid in passes of decreasing step length: N/2, N/4, N/8, … 1. Each cell stores the nearest seed found so far. After O(log N) passes, every cell knows its closest seed — this is dramatically faster than the brute-force O(n·k) approach for large seed counts. Weighted Voronoi (power diagram) subtracts a per-seed weight from the distance, producing curved boundaries that grow/shrink individual cells. Logistic Map Bifurcation

The logistic map — xn+1 = r · xn · (1 − xn) — is the simplest equation that produces the full spectrum of dynamical behavior: stable fixed points, period-doubling cascades, chaos, and periodic windows within the chaos. As the growth rate r sweeps from 2.5 to 4, the attractor splits, splits again, and ultimately erupts into fractal noise — the famous bifurcation diagram.

Move your mouse over the cobweb to change r in real time.

Bifurcation

Display

How this works

For each value of r along the x-axis, the logistic map is iterated: xn+1 = r · xn(1 − xn). After discarding transient iterations (warmup), the remaining values are plotted as dots at (r, xn). When r < 3 the attractor is a single fixed point. At r = 3 it splits into a 2-cycle, then 4, 8, 16… in a geometric cascade governed by the Feigenbaum constant δ ≈ 4.669 — the ratio of successive bifurcation intervals converges to this universal number, the same for every unimodal map. The diagram is self-similar: zooming into any bifurcation reveals miniature copies of the whole structure.

experiment 29

Traveling Salesman Problem

The Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) is the most famous combinatorial optimization problem: given a set of cities, find the shortest possible route that visits each city exactly once and returns to the origin. It is NP-hard — there is no known polynomial-time algorithm for the optimal solution, so we rely on heuristics. Here we solve it with simulated annealing using 2-opt moves, watching the tour evolve from a random tangle into a near-optimal Hamiltonian cycle in real time.

Click on the canvas to add cities. Press Solve to start the annealing process. The temperature starts high (accepting almost any move) and cools geometrically — worse moves become increasingly unlikely until the tour freezes into a local optimum. The current tour (solid line) and the best tour found so far (ghost overlay) are drawn live.

TSP · Ready · tour: — · best: — · T: — · — fps

Simulated Annealing

Display

How this works

Starting from a random tour, simulated annealing mimics the physical annealing of metals. At each step a random 2-opt move is proposed: two edges of the tour are removed and the resulting paths are reconnected in the other possible way, which reverses a segment of the tour. If the move shortens the tour it is always accepted. If it lengthens the tour, it is accepted with probability e−ΔE/T, where ΔE is the distance increase and T is the current temperature. This allows the algorithm to escape local minima early on.

The temperature is multiplied by the cooling rate each step. A rate of 0.9995 cools slowly (good quality, slower); a rate of 0.99 cools fast (quick but may freeze suboptimally). When temperature approaches zero the system freezes and the tour stabilizes. The Metropolis acceptance criterion is the core of the Kirkpatrick et al. (1983) annealing algorithm.

experiment 30

Cyclic Cellular Automaton

A cyclic cellular automaton (CCA) is a discrete model where each cell holds a state from 0 to N−1. A cell in state k advances to state k+1 if at least T of its neighbors are already in state k+1; state N−1 wraps back to 0. Despite these trivial rules, the system spontaneously generates rotating spirals, expanding target waves, and chaotic shimmering patterns — the same dynamics seen in excitable media like heart tissue, Belousov-Zhabotinsky reactions, and slime mold networks.

Click or drag on the canvas to seed new wavefronts. Lower threshold values produce dense, chaotic patterns; higher thresholds create smooth, organized spirals. Adjust the number of states to change the color palette depth and wave complexity.

CCA · Spiral · step: 0 · — fps

Automaton Rules

Display

How this works

The cyclic cellular automaton was introduced by David Griffeath in 1988. Each cell has a state in {0, 1, …, N−1}. At each time step, a cell in state k looks at its neighbors: if at least T of them are in state (k+1) mod N, the cell increments to that state. Otherwise it stays put. No other rule — no diffusion, no probability, no external forcing.

What emerges depends critically on the threshold T and the number of states N. With low thresholds (T=1–2) the system quickly fills with chaotic, shimmering noise. With moderate thresholds (T=3) and enough states (N≥10), beautiful rotating spiral waves self-organize from random initial conditions — each spiral core acts as a pacemaker, emitting expanding ring waves that collide and annihilate with waves from neighboring cores. With high thresholds (T≥5), only compact wavefronts survive, producing labyrinthine network patterns. The von Neumann neighborhood (4 neighbors) tends to produce more angular, crystalline patterns compared to the organic spirals of the Moore neighborhood.

experiment 31

Marching Squares & Metaballs

Metaballs are organic, smoothly-merging blobs defined by a scalar field — a function that is high inside each ball and falls off with distance. Where two balls approach, their fields overlap and the isosurface (the set of points where the field equals a threshold) flows together into a single connected shape, creating the classic "liquid metal" effect.

The contour is extracted per cell using the Marching Squares algorithm: each 2×2 cell of the field grid is classified by which corners exceed the threshold (16 possible cases), and line segments are placed accordingly. Linear interpolation along the edges produces smooth, continuous contours. Click to spawn new blobs, drag to move them, and watch them merge and split as they drift.

Metaballs · Classic Blobs · blobs: 8 · — fps

Metaball Physics

Rendering

How this works

Each metaball i at position (xᵢ, yᵢ) with radius rᵢ contributes a field value fᵢ(x,y) = rᵢ² / ((x−xᵢ)² + (y−yᵢ)² + ε). The total field at any point is the sum of all blob contributions. The isosurface at threshold T is the set of points where the total field equals T — this is what gives metaballs their smooth, organic merging behavior: when two balls are far apart, they form separate circles, but as they approach, their fields overlap and the surface smoothly bridges them into one shape.

Marching Squares extracts this contour efficiently. The field is sampled on a regular grid; each 2×2 block of grid samples is classified by which of its 4 corners exceed the threshold (16 possible configurations). For each configuration, the algorithm knows exactly which cell edges the contour crosses, and linear interpolation along those edges places the crossing points precisely. The result is a smooth, continuous polyline contour rendered at any resolution. Filled mode uses the same segments but shades the interior; field mode renders the raw scalar field as a heat-map for debugging.

Maze Generation & Pathfinding

A maze is a perfect metaphor for algorithms: the same grid can be carved by wildly different strategies — recursive backtracking, Prim's growing tree, Wilson's random walks, or binary-tree carving — each producing mazes with a distinctive texture and bias. Once carved, the maze becomes a playground for pathfinding: BFS explores uniformly in all directions, Dijkstra follows cost gradients, and A* races toward the goal with its f = g + h heuristic. Watch how each solver explores the search space differently, and how maze topology shapes the frontier.

The grid uses a cell-with-walls model: each cell tracks four walls (N, E, S, W). Generators carve passages by removing walls between adjacent cells. Solvers treat the maze as a graph and animate their exploration frontier in real time. Click "Generate" for a new maze, pick a solver, and hit "Solve". Click cells to set start (green) and goal (red) points.

Maze · Recursive Backtracker · ready · — fps

Maze Parameters

Display

How this works

Generators all produce a perfect maze — one with no loops and exactly one path between any two cells. Recursive Backtracking does a random walk, carving passages and backtracking at dead-ends, producing long winding corridors. Prim's grows outward from a random seed, always picking a random frontier wall to remove, yielding short corridors and many junctions. Wilson's performs loop-erased random walks from arbitrary cells until they hit the growing tree, producing uniform spanning trees. Binary Tree and Sidewinder are O(n) one-pass algorithms with strong directional bias.

Solvers explore the maze graph: BFS (Breadth-First Search) radiates outward in all directions, guaranteeing the shortest path on unweighted graphs. DFS (Depth-First Search) dives deep before backtracking — fast but not optimal. Dijkstra's algorithm generalizes BFS to weighted graphs using a priority queue. A* enhances Dijkstra with a heuristic (here, Manhattan distance to the goal), dramatically reducing the explored area by pulling the frontier toward the target. Greedy Best-First goes further — it uses only the heuristic, racing toward the goal but possibly taking a non-optimal route.

Chladni Plate Patterns

When a metal plate sprinkled with sand is vibrated at a resonant frequency, the sand gathers along the nodal lines — places where the plate barely moves — forming intricate geometric patterns. This is the experiment Ernst Chladni demonstrated to Napoleon in 1808, and it's the first experiment on this page that makes sound: turn your volume up and play the actual frequency that shapes the plate.

Each mode shape is W(x,y) = cos(mπx) · cos(nπy) for a square plate. Particles drift toward the nodes (where |W| ≈ 0) and away from the antinodes (where |W| is largest). Adjust m and n to change the mode, or combine modes for Chladni's classic figures.

Chladni · Mode (1,2) · 80 Hz · — fps

Mode Shape

Simulation

How this works

For a square plate with fixed edges, the standing-wave solution of the wave equation ∇²W = −λW is W(x,y) = cos(mπx) · cos(nπy), where m and n are the number of half-wavelengths along each axis. The lines where W(x,y) = 0 (the nodal lines) are where sand settles, since those points don't oscillate. The interior of each "cell" between nodal lines vibrates up and down, throwing sand outward.

The simulation scatters thousands of particles across the plate and nudges each one away from high-amplitude regions (where |W| is large) and toward nodal lines (where |W| ≈ 0). A small amount of noise keeps the motion organic. When you press Play Sound, the Web Audio API synthesizes the actual resonant frequency — approximately f = f₀ · √(m² + n²) — so you can hear the plate vibrate. Different (m, n) pairs produce the classic Chladni figures that stunned 19th-century audiences.

experiment 34

Penrose Tiling Generator

A Penrose tiling is a non-periodic tiling of the plane that never repeats yet has fivefold rotational symmetry. Roger Penrose discovered these in 1974; Dan Shechtman found them in nature (quasicrystals) in 1982 and won the 2011 Nobel Prize for it. This generator builds them by recursive subdivision of golden triangles — each step splits every triangle into smaller ones using the golden ratio φ = (1+√5)/2, producing patterns that are ordered but never repeat.

The P3 tiling uses two triangle types: a thin "red" triangle with angles 36°-72°-72° and a thick "blue" triangle with angles 72°-36°-72°. Each subdivides into one or more smaller triangles of both types. After n subdivisions the ratio of red to blue triangles approaches 1/φ.

Penrose · Sun · Level 5 · 130 tiles · — fps

Tiling

Appearance

How this works

The P3 Penrose tiling is built from two golden triangles: a thin triangle (36°-72°-72°, "red") and a thick triangle (72°-36°-72°, "blue"). Each triangle subdivides into smaller copies of both types using the golden ratio φ = (1+√5)/2 ≈ 1.618:

Red → 1 red + 1 blue
Blue → 2 blue + 1 red

After n subdivisions, the red-to-blue ratio converges to 1/φ, and the total tile count grows by a factor of φ² ≈ 2.618 per level. The key property is that these substitution rules cannot produce a periodic tiling — the only way to tile the plane with these triangles is non-periodically, yet the result has global fivefold symmetry. This is impossible with ordinary crystals (the crystallographic restriction forbids 5-fold symmetry), which is why quasicrystals were so controversial.

Try it: start with Sun and subdivide to level 5–6 for the classic star pattern. Switch to Star for a different initial arrangement. Use the orientation color mode to see the 10 angular classes. Click the canvas to pan, or Subdivide +1 to zoom deeper into the tiling.
